How to Determine Your Resource Needs by Analyzing Past Jobs

One of the best ways to improve your future resource requests is to learn from your past jobs. By checking the actual resources a completed job used, you can make more accurate requests, which helps your jobs start sooner and frees up resources for the entire UH community.

Koa provides several command-line tools to help you with this analysis.

Tools for Analyzing Your Job History

Here’s a quick guide to the tools available on Koa, from the simplest to the most detailed. For all examples, replace <job_id> with the actual ID of a completed job.

Quick Comparison of Tools

Tool Best For... Output Format Complexity
seff A quick, easy efficiency summary. Simple Text Low
jobstats A detailed, visual analysis over time. HTML Report & Plots Medium
sacct Getting raw, comprehensive accounting data. Detailed Text Table High

1. seff (Recommended Starting Point)

This is the easiest and most useful tool for a quick review. It provides a simple, human-readable summary of your job's efficiency.

  • Best For: Getting a quick, "at-a-glance" report on a completed job's performance.

  • How to Use:

    seff <jobid>

  • What to Look For: Pay close attention to the CPU Efficiency and Memory Efficiency percentages. These tell you if you requested the right amount of CPUs and RAM.

2. jobstats

This tool provides a much more detailed, visual analysis of your job's performance over time.

  • Best For: Troubleshooting an inefficient job. Use this when seff reports low efficiency and you need to understand why.

  • How to Use:

    jobstats <jobid>

  • Web Dashboard instructions

    • https://go.hawaii.edu/Tym 

3. sacct

This is a powerful, built-in Slurm command that provides a comprehensive and detailed accounting of all job information. Its output can be overwhelming, so it is best used when you need specific, raw data that seff doesn't provide.

  • Best For: Advanced users who need very specific, detailed, and raw accounting data about a past job.

  • How to Use:

# The "-l" flag provides a long, detailed output
sacct -j <jobid> -l | less -S

A Practical Guide to Requesting Resources

Requesting the right amount of resources for your job is a critical skill. It not only helps your own research but also benefits the entire UH community. Here’s a breakdown of the consequences of over-requesting and under-requesting for each major resource type.

At-a-Glance Summary: The Impact of Over-Requesting

If you request too much... The primary negative impact is...
Time Your job may wait longer in the queue because it's harder to schedule.
CPU Cores Your job priority will be lowered for future jobs, making you wait longer next time.
CPU Memory Your job priority will be lowered for future jobs, making you wait longer next time.
GPUs Your job priority is lowered, and your job may be automatically canceled if GPUs are left unused.

In-Depth Guide to Resource Requests

1. Requesting Time (--time)

  • Requesting Too Much: Your job will be harder for the scheduler to fit into available slots ("backfilling"). This means you will likely spend more time waiting in the queue, especially when the cluster is busy.

  • Requesting Too Little: This is critical. If your job exceeds the time you requested, it will be immediately canceled and all progress will be lost.

  • Best Practice: Analyze a past job with seff to see how long it took. For your next run, request that amount of time plus a reasonable buffer (e.g., 20%) to account for any variability.

2. Requesting CPU Cores (--cpus-per-task)

  • Requesting Too Much: Requesting more CPU cores than your application can use will not make it run faster. However, your job priority "account" is charged for every core you reserve. This lowers your priority for future jobs, increasing your wait times later.

  • Requesting Too Little: Your application may run slower than its full potential.

  • Best Practice: Match the number of CPUs to the number of threads or parallel processes your application is designed to use. Use seff to check your "CPU Efficiency" and adjust accordingly.

3. Requesting CPU Memory (--mem)

  • Requesting Too Much: Like with CPUs, this doesn't speed up your job but it does lower your priority for future jobs, as you are charged for all the memory you reserve.

  • Requesting Too Little: This is critical. If your job tries to use more memory than you requested, it will be immediately canceled with an "Out of Memory" (OOM) error.

  • Best Practice: Use seff on a completed job to see its peak "Memory Utilized." For your next run, request that amount plus a safe buffer (15-20%) to prevent unexpected crashes.

4. Requesting GPUs (--gres)

  • Requesting Too Much: You will lower your priority for future jobs. More importantly, Koa's community partitions have a strict policy: if your job requests multiple GPUs but leaves any of them completely idle for a period of time, it will be automatically canceled after you receive a series of warning emails.

  • Requesting Too Little: Your application will not be able to use the GPUs it needs.

  • Best Practice: Only request the exact number of GPUs your application is written to use. If your code is not GPU-enabled, do not request any GPUs.
