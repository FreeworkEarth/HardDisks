\title{Where injected work goes in a hard-disk box:\\
a proof ladder for an energy-transfer experiment}

\author{Christoph~Haring and Susanne~Still%
\thanks{The authors are with the Department of Information and Computer Sciences,
University of Hawai\`i at M\=anoa, Honolulu, HI 96822 USA
(e-mail: charing@hawaii.edu; sstill@hawaii.edu).}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
A piston compresses a hard-disk gas in an isolated box. We ask what fraction of the injected work
can still be recovered as ordered energy, and we refuse to answer until every simpler statement
below it is closed: the ledger, the quasi-static baseline, the finite-rate excess, the spring, the
divider. Each level is stated with its pass criterion before it is measured. We find that a closed
compartment has \emph{no} linear friction --- the force autocorrelation of a held piston integrates
to zero at the sound traversal time --- and that the finite-rate cost is second order,
$\avg W - W_{\mathrm{qs}} = Au^2$, of which roughly half is energy the protocol itself launches.
A spring-loaded wall is then shown to store work at \emph{first} order in its displacement, because
it is pre-loaded, and a parameter-free model whose gas spring is Paper 1's measured sound speed
predicts where that wall settles. Two levels failed on their first attempt for reasons that were
statistical rather than physical, and we report both.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
hard-disk fluid, energy transfer, event-driven molecular dynamics, dissipation, adiabatic piston,
fluctuation theorems
\end{IEEEkeywords}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{I}{f} a piston compresses a gas in a box with no heat bath, where does the work go?
Part raises the gas temperature irreversibly; part can in principle be recovered by a load. The
split is the quantity of interest for any engine argument --- Szilard's included~\cite{szilard1929}
--- and for the bounds relating dissipation to protocol speed~\cite{jarzynski1997,sivak2012}.

The difficulty is not the simulation but the bookkeeping. At the sizes where a hard-disk box is
cheap, $N \sim 100$, thermal fluctuations are $O(\sqrt N\,\kT)$ and comparable to the effects being
measured; a statistic chosen carelessly will find structure that is not there. We therefore
organise the work as a \emph{ladder}: each level states an observable and a pass criterion before
the runs, and no level is trusted until the one below it is closed. Two of the levels below failed
first time for statistical reasons and are reported with the failure, because the failures are the
reusable part.

The ladder is tied to a companion measurement. Paper 1 measures the speed of sound of the same gas
from the eigenmode of a massive divider~\cite{roman2002}; the heavy-divider limit of that mode gives
a gas spring of stiffness
\begin{equation}
k_{\mathrm{gas}} = \frac{N m c_s^2}{L^2},
\label{eq:kgas}
\end{equation}
which is exactly the spring that appears in Level 3 below. The two papers share this one line of
physics, and Paper 1's measured $c_s$ enters Level 3 as an input.

\section{Apparatus}\label{sec:apparatus}
All levels run in one box, with elements switched on rather than rebuilt
(Fig.~\ref{fig:geoms}). An empty compartment behind a held wall carries the spring; two gas
compartments of $N_s = 50$ disks each, $38.75\,\sigma$ long and $H = 10$ high, give
$\eta = 0.1013$; a piston on the right is the only place work enters. Units are reduced,
$\kT = m = \sigma = 1$. The four geometries are
\begin{itemize}\itemsep1pt
\item[A] both walls held --- Levels 0--2;
\item[B] divider free --- Level 4;
\item[C] divider removed, one gas of 100 disks driving the spring-loaded wall --- Level 3;
\item[D] both free --- the chain.
\end{itemize}

The dynamics are event-driven and exact between collisions. Every trajectory is checked against a
health contract --- a scheduler forced advance, an overlap repair, a wall clamp or an overdue wall
collision each discard that trajectory. Every campaign reported here ran with zero health events.

\section{Level 0 --- the ledger closes}\label{sec:l0}
The energy residual
$R_E = W - [\Delta KE_{\mathrm{gas}} + \Delta KE_{\mathrm{walls}} + \Delta E_{\mathrm{spring}} + Q]$
and the matching momentum residual are formed from the per-event impulse log.
\textbf{Measured:} $|R_E|/W \le 8\times10^{-12}$ and $|R_p|/\sum|\Delta p| \le 1\times10^{-13}$ over
all 75 pilot trajectories. \textbf{Passed.}

\section{Level 2 --- the finite-rate excess}\label{sec:l2}
\paragraph{Slow end} With $\zeta(\lambda) = \beta\int_0^\infty \avg{\delta F(0)\delta F(t)}\dd t$
and $W_{\mathrm{diss}} \simeq \zeta u \Delta x$, the integral is \emph{not} Markovian. It falls from
$2.55$ at zero lag --- the Enskog collisional friction --- and is cancelled by the returning sound
wave at $L/c_s = 19.8\,\sigma$, confirmed at the compressed end of the path where the crossing moves
to $18.0\,\sigma$ against a predicted $15.9$. Its zero-frequency limit is the hydrodynamic viscous
friction $\zeta_{\mathrm{hyd}} = L_y\Gamma/X_p = 0.081 \pm 0.010$, predicting a linear term
$0.32 \pm 0.04\,u$ --- below the resolution of 760 trajectories and consistent with the measured
slope $-0.50 \pm 0.67$ for $u \le 0.05$. $\Gamma$ is taken from Paper 1's divider linewidth, not
assumed: $\Gamma = 0.314 \pm 0.038$ against the Enskog value $0.331$.
% TODO-source: 260917_paper2_level2_REPORT.md sections 2-4; 260918_A1v2_linewidth.json

\textbf{What is there instead is quadratic:} $A_{\mathrm{step}} = 25.9 \pm 4.3$ against
$A_{\mathrm{ramp}} = 11.3 \pm 2.5$ when the piston is ramped over $2L/c_s$ instead of stepped
($3.0\sigma$ apart). Roughly $14$ of the $25$ is energy the step protocol launches; what a gentle
push leaves is consistent with the steady-flow value $N_s m/6 = 8.3$.

\paragraph{Fast end} The single-hit law $\avg W \simeq 2N_s m(\Delta x/L)u^2$~\cite{lua2005} has no
free parameter and is met to $0.986$ at $u = 10$, $\Delta x = 7.08\,\sigma$. The apparent $8\,\%$
excess at short travel is the contact layer: the strip a face can strike (width $\Delta x$ starting
at face $-\,r$) holds $1.13\times$ the uniform-density count at $\Delta x = 0.75\,\sigma$. Using the
measured count the ratio becomes $0.96$--$1.03$ with no trend.

\section{Level 3 --- can a spring capture it?}\label{sec:l3}
\paragraph{The apparatus is a pre-loaded spring} A spring holding a wall against a gas is not at its
natural length: it carries the standing force $F = N\kT Z/L$, so the wall sits $d = F/k$ from the
anchor and a further displacement $s$ stores
\begin{equation}
\Delta E(s) = F s + \tfrac12 k s^2 ,
\label{eq:preload}
\end{equation}
which is \textbf{first} order in $s$. The $Fs$ term is work lifting a weight. The limits fix the
shape: $k \to 0$ is a pushrod ($\epsilon \to 1$ trivially, with nothing taken from the gas), and
$k \to \infty$ a rigid wall ($\epsilon \to 0$).

\paragraph{The gas spring is adiabatic, and it is Paper 1's $c_s$} There is no heat bath, so
compression heats the gas. With $U = N\kT$ in two dimensions, $\dd U = -P\,\dd V$ gives
$\dd\ln T/\dd\ln L = -Z$ and hence \eqref{eq:kgas} --- the same expression as the heavy-divider
limit of Paper 1's mode. It is $2.01\times$ the isothermal stiffness here, and the adiabat
reproduces Level 1's measured $T_f/T_i$ to $0.5\,\%$.

\paragraph{The observable, and a lesson} The first analysis reported a large transient excess over
the model and attributed it to the acoustic pulse. It was neither. The statistic was a maximum over
time of a noisy ensemble mean, and a \emph{no-push control} --- the same apparatus with the piston
never started --- reproduces it: at $u = 0.02$ the push gives $+0.463$ and $+0.339\,\sigma$ above
settled while the control gives $+0.446$ and $+0.354$. The correct observable is the \emph{settled}
displacement, and the peak is retained only as a diagnostic.
% TODO-source: 260923_paper2_level3_REPORT_v5.md sections 1-2

\paragraph{Result} Pooled over five cells with validated windows,
$\bar s = 0.8705 \pm 0.0049\,\sigma$. The gas input is the box's own measured pressure: in
equilibrium the force a hard wall feels \emph{is} the pressure of the box
($P = \kT\rho_{\mathrm{contact}}$, with the normal pressure uniform), so
$Z_{\mathrm{box}}(\eta) = FL/(N\kT)$ is read directly from the momentum delivered to a held wall,
with the gas temperature measured at the same instant. With it the parameter-free model
$M\ddot s = F(L - x_p + s) - F(L) - ks$ predicts the settled displacement to
$\mathbf{-1.3 \pm 0.6\,\%}$ ($2.2\sigma$); with the bulk equation of state, $+3.6\,\%$
(Fig.~\ref{fig:l3settled}). \textbf{Level 3 closes on the observable.}

\section{Level 4 --- transmission through a free divider}\label{sec:l4}
Geometry B: the piston compresses one gas, a free divider of mass $M_d$ separates it from the other.
Quasi-statically the divider moves to pressure balance, so at first order each gas takes half the
volume change and half the work, and the divider settles at $\Delta x/2$.

\paragraph{A protocol fix found by the pilot} The first pilot could not measure its own observable.
The trace schema assigns segment 0 to one kinetic-energy column and \emph{every other segment} to
the other, so in the master box --- whose segment 0 is the empty spring compartment --- the columns
read ``zero'' and ``both gases combined''. Level 4 therefore runs in a two-compartment box, where
the columns are the two gases; an empty compartment behind a held wall is already known to change
nothing the gas can see (\S\ref{sec:l0}).
% TODO-source: 260925_paper2_level4_PILOT.md section 2

\paragraph{Pilot results} At $M_d = 50$ the work split is $0.502 \pm 0.140$ of the total, i.e.
$0.0\sigma$ from one half, and the divider settles at $|{-2.001}| \pm 0.184\,\sigma$ against the
predicted $1.965$, $0.2\sigma$. The error bars are set by the per-gas kinetic-energy fluctuation,
$O(\sqrt{N_s})\,\kT$ per seed, which is why a $2.1\sigma$ departure seen at $M_d = 200$ on 20 seeds
is not yet evidence of a mass dependence.

\paragraph{How long the divider takes} This is the adiabatic-piston problem, named as an open
question by Lieb in 1998~\cite{cencini2007}. Stage 1 is a damped oscillation to \emph{mechanical}
equilibrium, $P_l \simeq P_r$ with $T_l \neq T_r$, governed by $R = mN/M$ --- over-damped for
$R > \mathcal{O}(1)$, weakly damped below~\cite{cencini2007,crosignani1996}. Here $R = 5.00$ and
$1.00$ at $M_d = 10$ and $50$. Stage 2 is a Brownian drift along the isobar toward $T_l = T_r$, on a
timescale \emph{proportional to $M$}~\cite{cencini2007}; linearising Gruber and Piasecki's
stationary velocity~\cite{gruber1999} gives $\tau_T = (4/\sqrt{2\pi})ML_c/\sqrt{mk_BT}$, independent
of $N$ and of density. Both papers assume an ideal gas; carrying $Z(\eta)$ through the isobar
multiplies the rate by $1 + \eta Z'/Z = 1.228$, so $\tau_T = 50.4\,M$ against the ideal $61.8\,M$ ---
a $23\,\%$ gap the experiment can resolve.

Starting the two gases at $T = 1.25$ and $0.75$ with the divider free and no piston, stage 1 is
observed. Taking the centre of the oscillation over three periods and correcting for stage-2 decay,
the offset is $3.678 \pm 0.174$ and $3.771 \pm 0.139\,\sigma$ at $M_d = 10$ and $50$, against
$3.989$ carrying $Z(\eta)$ through the isobar and $4.922$ for an ideal gas: $1.8\sigma$ and
$1.6\sigma$ from the first, $7\sigma$ and $8\sigma$ from the second. \textbf{The hard-disk
correction to the isobar is confirmed in stage 1.}

\paragraph{Stage 2 from equilibrium fluctuations} A decaying step is the wrong instrument here. The
per-seed spread of $T_1 - T_2$ is not measurement noise: hard disks carry no potential energy, so a
compartment's kinetic temperature \emph{is} its energy, the microcanonical split is
$f = E_1/E \sim \mathrm{Beta}(N,N)$, and $\sigma(T_1-T_2) = 4\bar T/(2\sqrt{2N+1}) = 0.199$ ---
measured $\mathbf{0.1980}$. Relaxation and fluctuation being one process, the \emph{stationary}
autocorrelation carries $\tau_T$ with no step to lose against that floor.

It is not a single exponential. Its spectrum holds $45\,\%$ of the variance at periods
$>200\,\sigma$-time, a discrete oscillation at period $95.3$ carrying $\approx 30\,\%$, and a fast
remainder; fitting one exponential across all three returns values that differ by $3.5\sigma$
between the two observables of the same process. Block-averaging at $100\,\sigma$-time removes the
oscillation and they agree. Calibrating the identical procedure on Ornstein--Uhlenbeck series of
known $\tau$ --- same record, blocking, fit window and seed count --- gives a response slope of
$0.335$, and inverting it,
\[
\tau_T(M_d\!=\!10) = 490 \pm 190\ (T_1{-}T_2),\qquad 450 \pm 165\ (\text{divider }x),
\]
against $503$ for hard disks and $618$ ideal. This is the first direct measurement of the stage-2
timescale in an interacting gas, and it is consistent with both: a $23\,\%$ gap is not resolvable
with a $35$--$40\,\%$ error bar. Resolving it is a question of \emph{record length} rather than
seeds, because the estimator's bias is common-mode and seeds do not touch it --- quadrupling the
record lifts the response slope from $0.29$ to $0.85$, and $65\,\tau_T$ at 80 seeds separates the
two hypotheses at $3.3\sigma$.

The oscillation at period $95.3$ is unexplained: the adiabatic gas spring
$k_{\mathrm{gas}} = N\kT c_s^2/L^2$ predicts $44.7$, and it is not a release transient. It is
reported rather than accounted for.

Measuring this in an \emph{interacting} gas is the point: Cencini \emph{et al.} call that case
``still too ambitious'' and note that very few studies have attempted it~\cite{cencini2007}.

\section{Finite size}\label{sec:finitesize}
The box is $\mathbf{2.5\,\%}$ over-pressured relative to Kolafa--Rottner and $\mathbf{1.0\,\%}$
over-stiff in $c_s$ (Paper 1, same $\eta$). Through $c_s^2 = Z + \eta Z' + Z^2$ the first would
imply $+1.9\,\%$ in $c_s$, so the two do not agree, at roughly $3\sigma$. These are two
\emph{different} finite-size numbers for the same $N = 100$ box, and the bulk relation between them
does not hold; both are reported, and neither is used to explain the other. The same statement
appears in Paper 1 from the sound-speed side.

\section{Outlook}
The Level 4 ladder in $M_d$ is the immediate continuation, and the equilibrium route sets its cost:
records of $\approx 65\,\tau_T$ at 80 seeds, i.e. $3.3\times10^4$, $1.6\times10^5$ and
$6.6\times10^5\,\sigma$-time at $M_d = 10, 50, 200$. That ladder tests the \emph{exponent} in
$\tau_T \propto M$, which a single mass cannot, and at $65\,\tau_T$ it also separates the hard-disk
and ideal isobars in stage 2 as stage 1 already does. A stationary alternative --- outer walls held
at two temperatures, conductivity from the steady flux --- would remove the finite-record bias
entirely, at the price of a per-wall heat-bath temperature and per-wall energy accounting, which the
present single-global bath does not provide. Beyond it, Level 5 asks whether a chain of walls beats a single one --- the prediction
there is an \emph{ordering}, with geometrically matched intermediate masses beating badly matched
ones --- and Level 6 asks for the information bound,
$\beta\avg{W_{\mathrm{diss}}} \ge I_{\mathrm{mem}} - I_{\mathrm{pred}}$~\cite{still2012}, which
requires a canonical seeder and the finite-box $\Delta F$ as the Jarzynski benchmark rather than the
bulk one.

Raw trajectories are not in the repository; each cell carries its summary and the exact command,
and Level 2 an 85\,kB digest from which the whole analysis reproduces. Every binary change carries a
